<Sec label="Context" title="The school wanted one place for alumni to learn and connect.">

Dean Unnava wanted to support alumni's professional success long after graduation. The GSM could offer professional certificates and course content, but before designing any of it, we needed to understand how alumni approach learning, support and staying connected.

</Sec>

<Sec label="Research" title="We started with how alumni actually learn.">

<Principles columns="3">
<Pr title="Focus groups">

Alumni expectations for career support, content relevance and community connection.

</Pr>
<Pr title="User interviews">

Upskilling habits, learning formats and how much they trust GSM content.

</Pr>
<Pr title="Quantitative survey">

The platforms they use, willingness to pay, pricing preferences and content access.

</Pr>
</Principles>

<Frame src="/images/case-studies/uc-davis-gsm/affinity-board-framed.webp" alt="Affinity map grouping alumni research notes by motivations, certifications, GSM events, networking and how they like to learn" width="2406" height="936" />

</Sec>

<Sec label="Core themes" title="Three themes became the platform's pillars.">

<Principles columns="3">
<Pr title="Community and support">

Alumni want transparent communication and to feel genuinely engaged after graduating, not just reached out to when it's convenient.

</Pr>
<Pr title="Networking and mentorship">

Alumni want easier ways to connect professionally beyond their existing circles, for mentorship, jobs and industry ties.

</Pr>
<Pr title="Learning">

Alumni want credible, time-efficient content that keeps them competitive and current.

</Pr>
</Principles>

</Sec>

<Sec label="The problem" title="Alumni needed more than content.">

*How might we create a centralized platform where alumni and students can access high-quality learning content, engage in meaningful professional conversations, and keep building a supportive GSM community for lifelong growth?*

</Sec>

<Sec label="Lo-fi to mid-fi" title="Core solutions first, branding second.">

In lo-fi I got the core solutions on screen and only lightly refined lower-priority elements. At mid-fi I added branding, copy and interaction detail, so alumni unfamiliar with UX could test the Figma prototype and give focused feedback on the key features.

<Sec label="Usability testing" title="Testing turned three problems into structural decisions.">

We tested the mid-fi prototype with alumni and rebuilt what tripped them up. Three problems ran deeper than a single screen.

<Principles columns="3">
<Pr title="Search had no clear purpose">

Was it for people or content?

I split it into a focused search on the learning page, with a top nav pointing straight to the directory and forum.

</Pr>
<Pr title="Saved and published content had no home">

Alumni couldn't find what they had saved or posted.

I added a bookmark icon linking to saved content, and moved uploading onto the learning homepage so every learning action sits in one place.

</Pr>
<Pr title="The geomap was buried">

It sat inside advanced search.

I moved it to a single map-expansion button, front and center.

</Pr>
</Principles>

<Sec label="Reflection" title="Pushing Figma and learning from PMs.">

<Lesson title="Figma has limits.">

Building the advanced filter pushed Figma to its edge. Without conditionals or multiple actions, I couldn't fully simulate the interaction, and that limited what usability testing could tell us. I still came out with interactive maps, scroll buttons and smart component swaps in my toolkit, and my first 8pt grid.

</Lesson>

<Lesson title="PMs are magic.">

Watching our PMs work, from roadmaps in Notion to feature scoping and team alignment, gave me a deep respect for cross-functional collaboration.

</Lesson>

Thank you to my team, AggieWorks, and Dean Unnava and the GSM for trusting us with this.
