<Intro>

Clients struggled to finish the event request form, and event programmers kept chasing them for what was missing. The form was built like the Salesforce backend instead of for the person filling it in. I rebuilt it around what clients actually know, going from seven pages to three. EP leads in two countries helped shape it, and it was handed off for build.

</Intro>

<Section label="Context">

<div className="content-block">

### Clients were filling out a form that was written for someone else.

Event Programmers (EPs) build every event on Ticketmaster in Salesforce. Clients send them what they need through an event request form on the Support Community.

That form was structured like the Salesforce backend, because it feeds straight into it. The backend is where EPs enter event dates, event codes, artist info and more. But the client-facing form had been laid out the same way. Clients saw a pile of inputs that only EPs needed, and many of them couldn't be filled in by a client at all.

</div>

<CardRow columns="3" spaced="true">
  <InsightCard num="Drop-off">Clients gave up partway through the form.</InsightCard>
  <InsightCard num="Gaps">Events arrived with wrong or missing details, so they couldn't be fully built.</InsightCard>
  <InsightCard num="Back and forth">EPs chased clients by email to fill in the gaps.</InsightCard>
</CardRow>

<p className="label">What the project team was seeing, across the smaller EMEA markets</p>

</Section>

<Section label="The Problem">

<div className="content-block">

### Every extra field cost clients time and cost EPs accuracy.

The project had four goals. Cut email between clients and the EP team by 15% in the first year. Cut event errors and escalations. Speed up EP processing, especially in markets with a lot of events to build. Lift client and employee satisfaction, with a 5 point NPS gain.

The question I kept coming back to was this. *How might we make the form feel manageable for clients, while still capturing everything an EP needs to build the event?*

</div>

</Section>

<Section label="Where I Started">

<div className="content-block">

### Mapping the old form, field by field.

The old flow was seven pages, not counting pop-ups. I put every screen on one board and marked it up, shown below. Sylvaine, our CRM lead, had done user research, and her sticky notes sit right on the screens. The red marks are mine. Each one is a field or a step that leadership, client feedback or the data said we could cut. Some were clutter. Some were things a client had no way to know.

I also used Salesforce field usage data from Sylvaine. Fields that were rarely filled in came out, and the ones people did fill in stayed.

</div>

</Section>

<Section label="Principles">

<div className="content-block">

### Guiding principles

</div>

<CardRow columns="2">
  <InsightCard num="01">**Ask clients what clients know.** If only an EP can answer it, it doesn't belong on the client's form. EPs add the backend details themselves.</InsightCard>
  <InsightCard num="02">**Show a field only when it matters.** Single day or multi-day, GA or reserved seating, presale or not. The form changes with the answer, so nobody scrolls past fields that don't apply.</InsightCard>
  <InsightCard num="03">**Keep related things together.** Seven pages and pop-ups became three steps, with everything about tickets in one place.</InsightCard>
  <InsightCard num="04">**Let people check their work.** A progress stepper, save draft on every page, and a review step before anything is sent.</InsightCard>
</CardRow>

</Section>

<Section label="Step 1">

<div className="content-block">

### Event Info

The old form spread event info, presale details and venue selection over separate pages and pop-ups. They now sit on one page. The page asks for the basics, the dates and the venue. A toggle switches between a single day and multi-day event, and the multi-day option adds an end date. Event codes came out, since clients don't have them.

Presale moved here too, as a simple yes or no. EP lead Aimee suggested it, because the pricing details only matter once a client says there is a presale.

</div>

<div className="caption-block">

<p className="label">Decision and tradeoff</p>

The venue list is long. It already filters by market, but the better answer is to show only the venues tied to the client's records, so nobody scrolls through hundreds. That depended on what Salesforce could do, so I flagged it as "if technically possible" for the build team.

</div>

</Section>

<Section label="Step 2">

<div className="content-block">

### Venue and Ticket Details

This was the heaviest part of the old form, split across several pages and a pop-up for price types. It's now one page, in the order a client thinks about it. First the venue layout, then ticket categories and prices, then delivery, presales, blockings, and resale and transfers.

Fields appear as they're needed. Choosing reserved seating or GA changes the ticket fields. The presale section only opens when the client said there is a presale. Clients can add as many ticket categories as they need, and apply a fee to all of them at once.

</div>

<p className="label">Everything about tickets on one page</p>

</div>

</Section>

<Section label="Step 3">

<div className="content-block">

### Additional Details, then review

The last page collects supporting documents, notes for the EP team, and the support options for access operation and box office sales. The main button here says Final Review, not Submit, so clients know there is one more look before it goes out.

Clients can save a draft on any page and come back later. On the review page, each section shows what they entered with an edit option beside it. The goal is to catch mistakes before the EP has to.

</div>

</Section>

<Section label="Validation">

<div className="content-block">

### EP leads knew what was necessary.

I started with them, because they know which fields they truly need and which ones are noise. I walked them through the requirements and the mockups, and later sent a Figma prototype for comments. EP leads in the Netherlands and Germany gave detailed feedback.

</div>

<div className="caption-block">

<p className="label">What changed because of it</p>

The page called Ticket Delivery became Venue and Ticket Details, since that's what it really holds. I moved presale to the first page. I added sections the EPs said they couldn't get from clients any other way, like blockings, resale and transfers, and accessible tickets. Ticket categories can now carry several fees. Ticket Name became Ticket Category.

</div>

<div className="caption-block">

<p className="label">What's next</p>

Client field comprehension testing was planned as the next step, to check that clients understand each field the same way EPs do. It hadn't happened when the project was handed off.

</div>

</Section>

<div className="caption-block">

<p className="label">The handoff</p>

I handed the designs to our business analyst, who was going to work with the Salesforce developers on the build. Mockups alone don't say how a form behaves, so I also mapped it in a Google Sheet. Every row is one field, with its page, section, field type, any help text, and the front-end interaction. That covers things like which fields appear when a toggle changes, and which button adds another ticket type.

</div>

<Section label="Impact">

<div className="content-block">

### I can't give you results, and I won't invent them.

The form hadn't launched when I left, so there are no numbers to report. The project set its own measures up front. How many event requests come in through the form instead of email. How long a case takes to complete. Customer effort score and satisfaction after the event. Those are what the 15% email cut and the 5 point NPS gain would be measured against.

</div>

</Section>

<Section label="Reflection">

<div className="content-block">

### A form should be built around the person filling it in.

</div>

<div className="content-block">

#### The structure came from the system, not the client.

The old form looked like the Salesforce backend because it fed the backend. Starting from what clients know, and letting EPs add the rest, was the biggest change.

</div>

<div className="content-block">

#### EP leads first, clients next.

EP leads could tell me what was necessary and what wasn't, so I started with them. Clients are the check that comes after. Their feedback on the wording of each field is the part still to do.

</div>
